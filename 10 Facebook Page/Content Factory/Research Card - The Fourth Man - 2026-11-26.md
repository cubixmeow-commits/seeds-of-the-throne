## Verified record
- After the Endurance was lost, Shackleton, Captain Frank Worsley, and Tom Crean sailed the James Caird 800 miles to South Georgia, then crossed the island's unmapped mountains and glaciers on foot, a march of thirty-six hours, May 19 to 20, 1916, reaching the Stromness whaling station. (Expedition record; Bishop of London centenary service address, 2016.)
- Shackleton wrote in his own book, South (1919): "I know that during that long and racking march of thirty-six hours over the unnamed mountains and glaciers of South Georgia it seemed to me often that we were four, not three. I said nothing to my companions on the point, but afterwards Worsley said to me, 'Boss, I had a curious feeling on the march that there was another person with us.' Crean confessed to the same idea." (Quoted in full by the Royal Scottish Geographical Society and the Tate research publication.)
- None of the three spoke of it during the march; each believed he alone had felt it, until they compared accounts afterward. (RSGS.)
- T. S. Eliot took the account into The Waste Land (1922), "Who is the third who walks always beside you?", and his own footnote credits Shackleton's expedition account: explorers "at the extremity of their strength, had the constant delusion that there was one more member than could actually be counted." (RSGS, quoting Eliot's note.)
- The phenomenon was later named the Third Man factor; John Geiger's 2009 book of that name collected cases from mountaineers, solo sailors, and disaster survivors. (Tate research publication; expedition literature.)

## Honesty note
The mainstream explanation is exhaustion, cold, hunger, altitude, and sleeplessness acting on the brain; sensed-presence experiences are documented in extreme environments. The card reports what the three men said, in their leader's own words, and the skeptical reading stands beside it as its own paragraph. Nothing more is claimed.

## The verified record

- Daryl J. Bem, Cornell University professor of psychology, published
  "Feeling the Future: Experimental Evidence for Anomalous Retroactive
  Influences on Cognition and Affect" in the Journal of Personality and
  Social Psychology, 2011, vol. 100(3), pp. 407-425, the flagship journal
  of social psychology.
- The paper reports nine experiments, more than 1,000 participants, each
  one a standard laboratory effect run in reverse: the stimulus event
  happened after the response it was supposed to influence. Eight of the
  nine reached statistical significance; mean effect size d = 0.22.
- Experiment 1: 100 Cornell undergraduates guessed which of two curtains
  on a screen hid a picture. The computer selected which side held the
  picture only after the guess was logged. On erotic-image trials the
  students were right 53.1% of the time against a 50% chance baseline.
- Experiments 8 and 9, retroactive facilitation of recall: students
  studied a word list and took a recall test; only afterward did the
  computer randomly select a subset of the words for a practice exercise.
  Students recalled the words they were about to practice better than the
  words they were not.
- The replication record, stated plainly: three preregistered close
  replications of Experiment 9 (Ritchie, Wiseman and French, 2012) found
  nothing; Galak and colleagues (2012) ran seven experiments with
  thousands of participants and found no effect; across seventeen
  independent outside experiments the pooled effect is about d = 0.02.
  Bem and colleagues answered with a 2015 meta-analysis of 90 experiments
  from 33 laboratories in 14 countries, concluding the effect held.
- The paper is widely credited with helping trigger psychology's
  replication crisis and the reform movement that followed, including
  preregistration.

## Honesty note

This is a contested, unreplicated result, and the caption must carry that
as its own fair paragraph, not a footnote. The mainstream position today
is that the effect is not real and the paper exposed weak methods across
the field. The wonder that survives either way: the strongest journal in
psychology read the numbers and printed them, and the argument has never
fully closed, because the authors' own meta-analysis still stands on the
other side.

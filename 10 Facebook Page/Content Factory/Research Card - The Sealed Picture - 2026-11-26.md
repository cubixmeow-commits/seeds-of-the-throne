## Verified record
- From 1964 to 1978, at Maimonides Medical Center in Brooklyn, psychiatrist Montague Ullman and psychologist Stanley Krippner ran a sleep laboratory testing whether a sleeping person could pick up a picture being concentrated on in another room. (SPR Psi Encyclopedia, Maimonides Dream Telepathy Research.)
- The method: the sleeper was wired to an EEG machine. In a separate, sound-isolated room, a sender opened a sealed envelope holding one art print, chosen at random from a pool of twelve famous paintings, only after the sleeper was in bed. A buzzer told the sender when the sleeper entered dreaming sleep. The sleeper was woken after each dream period and described the dream into a tape recorder. (Ullman, "Bio-communication in dreams," J. Amer. Acad. Psychoanalysis, 1973.)
- The transcripts and the full pool of twelve pictures went to three outside judges who knew nothing about which picture belonged to which night, and ranked the matches blind. (Same source.)
- Of the first seven completed experiments, four produced statistically significant matches. In the seven-night follow-up pairing the best sleeper with the male sender, the judges' rankings were significant at the .001 level. (Ullman, Krippner and Feldstein, International Journal of Neuropsychiatry, 1966; Ullman 1973.)
- The work was published in mainstream journals, including the American Journal of Psychiatry (1970), and collected in the book Dream Telepathy (1973).

## Honesty note
The results were never settled science. Critics questioned the judging and whether the effect repeated reliably, and the laboratory's defenders answered that sleep-lab work was expensive and the field moved to simpler methods. The card reports the record and the dispute, and claims nothing more.

## Surviving anchor
The published record itself: the 1970 American Journal of Psychiatry paper and the 1973 book Dream Telepathy, which is where the protocols and transcripts live.

# Century-Scale Policy Experiments and Moral Stopping Rules

## Purpose

This report examines how the parent civilization could compare reconciliation, boundaries, cultivation, and evidentiary observation over a century without using “more evidence” to excuse unlimited harm. Adaptive clinical trials and safety monitoring provide structural analogies.

## Executive finding

A morally credible long-duration experiment needs predeclared hypotheses, continuous welfare monitoring, independent access to accumulating evidence, adaptation rules, and authority to stop for harm, demonstrated success, or futility.

The process should never require waiting for a villain's plan to reach maximum atrocity merely to prove what it was. Evidence can become sufficient before the final intended outcome occurs.

## 1. Long duration does not justify delayed monitoring

A century-scale design can measure institutional effects invisible in short trials: succession, generational narrative, path dependence, and repeated strategic adaptation. But long endpoints create a temptation to dismiss current harm as incomplete evidence.

Monitoring should occur at multiple timescales:

- immediate safety and rights violations;
- medium-term institutional capture;
- long-term cooperation and succession;
- cumulative burden across repeated environments;
- effects that appear only after apparent success.

## 2. Predeclared claims reduce retrospective storytelling

Before an environment begins, each school should state:

- expected mechanism;
- observable indicators;
- plausible alternative explanations;
- unacceptable harms;
- conditions for modification;
- conditions for stopping;
- what result would count as failure or futility.

Without this, every outcome can be reinterpreted as support. Escalation becomes proof that more reconciliation is needed, or proof that harsher boundaries were always right, depending on the observer.

## 3. Independent monitoring must see what sponsors cannot

Clinical-trial analogies use independent data and safety monitoring boards because investigators and sponsors may be invested in continuation. A planetary equivalent needs access to information unavailable to policy sponsors and authority to recommend or impose pause.

It should monitor:

- participant welfare;
- effects on nonparticipants;
- emerging benefits;
- evidence of futility;
- manipulation of records;
- whether consent remains valid;
- unequal distribution of harm;
- cumulative interaction among separate experiments.

## 4. Three different stopping reasons matter

### Harm

Observed danger exceeds authorized limits, even if the policy might eventually work.

### Success

Evidence becomes strong enough that withholding the better approach is no longer justified.

### Futility

The approach is unlikely to answer its question or achieve its purpose, making continued exposure unjustified.

Futility is especially useful for the colonization process. Repeating an accommodation that only expands the Throne's capacity may stop being an open question long before his final plan succeeds.

## 5. Adaptive design can preserve learning while reducing harm

Adaptive trials prospectively define how accumulating evidence can change allocation, sample size, or treatment arms. The parent civilization could similarly:

- reduce influence allocated to a failing policy;
- increase monitoring after specified warning signs;
- protect populations showing unusual vulnerability;
- add a new intervention without erasing comparison;
- suspend one mechanism while preserving others;
- require renewed consent after material changes.

Adaptation must be governed in advance. Improvised changes can become covert favoritism or manipulate outcomes.

## 6. Evidence thresholds should include moral evidence

Statistical certainty is not the only relevant standard. Some acts may establish intent, command responsibility, or unacceptable method even when long-term population outcomes remain uncertain.

Examples include:

- deliberate destruction of records;
- punishment of protected dissent;
- knowingly sending a subordinate into unwinnable harm;
- systematic circumvention of consent;
- capture of the monitoring system itself.

The system can conclude “this method is prohibited” without claiming perfect prediction of every future outcome.

## 7. The experiment must include its designers

The process generates evidence about the governing schools as well as contained leaders. Relevant questions include:

- Which school updates when predictions fail?
- Who minimizes harms caused by a favored policy?
- Who attempts to alter criteria after results?
- Who protects participants at cost to institutional prestige?
- Who treats uncertainty honestly?

This makes the colonization process a test of governance rather than a laboratory in which only the contained are judged.

## Failure modes

- Stopping rules exist but remain discretionary.
- Sponsors control the monitoring data.
- Each environment is evaluated separately while cumulative harm grows.
- Adaptation destroys comparability and is hidden afterward.
- “No final proof yet” becomes the answer to every warning.
- Early stopping protects the institution's reputation rather than participants.
- The system waits for completed evil when attempted or preparatory conduct is already sufficient.

## Creative questions

1. What specific threshold should have stopped the Throne earlier?
2. Who had access to the accumulating evidence but lacked authority?
3. Which school refuses a futility finding?
4. What adaptation accidentally gives the Throne a new opening?
5. What act makes further observation morally unnecessary?

## Bottom line for *Seeds of the Throne*

The century is valuable because institutions and ideologies reveal themselves slowly. It is not a license to observe forever. The system becomes morally serious when it can say, before the worst possible ending, that enough has been learned and continued exposure is no longer justified.

# Constrained Power and Civilization-Scale Governance

## Purpose

This report develops a governance architecture explaining why Sylvan, the Throne, George, their personal agents, and the planetary infrastructure can possess extraordinary capabilities without any one of them being able to impose the final outcome.

## Executive finding

The cleanest model is not a single numerical influence score. It is a layered authorization system in which power depends on:

- identity;
- role;
- domain;
- current context;
- affected parties;
- evidentiary confidence;
- historical standing;
- independent approvals;
- containment restrictions;
- escalation and audit rules.

The system can strongly assist action while refusing unilateral resolution. Conflict moves into the struggle to assemble valid authority, persuade agents, satisfy thresholds, and change the public facts on which permissions depend.

## 1. Separate capability from authority

A participant may know how to perform an action and possess the physical systems required while lacking authorization to use them against another protected participant.

This distinction creates four useful categories:

- **capable and authorized:** ordinary legitimate action;
- **capable but unauthorized:** dangerous pressure, attempted circumvention, or covert action;
- **authorized but incapable:** formal power requiring allies or infrastructure;
- **neither:** symbolic claims and bluff.

The Throne's mythology deliberately confuses capability, access, seniority, and legitimacy. Sylvan's evidence can reveal that early access never implied rightful authority.

## 2. Attribute-based decisions fit the setting

NIST's attribute-based access-control model provides a useful ancestor: authorization can depend on attributes of the subject, requested action, object, and environment. The same person can be authorized in one context and denied in another.

A planetary policy decision might evaluate:

- Who is requesting the action?
- Is the request personal, institutional, emergency, or custodial?
- Which people will be affected?
- Are those people participants, synthetics, dependents, or contained actors?
- What jurisdiction and role are active?
- Is consent available?
- Is the action reversible?
- What is the predicted harm?
- Which independent agents object?
- Is the contest private or in public phase?
- Is the evidence underlying the request authenticated?

Influence modifies how competing valid plans are weighted; it does not erase hard prohibitions.

## 3. Separation of duties prevents godlike control

Security governance uses separation of duties so that one user cannot misuse a system alone. High-consequence operations can require multiple independent roles at execution time.

The colonization system can divide functions among:

- identity authentication;
- plan evaluation;
- influence weighting;
- physical actuation;
- participant protection;
- evidence auditing;
- emergency intervention;
- public-phase authorization;
- parent-civilization oversight.

No personal Lumina or Daemon controls the whole chain. An agent can advocate, predict, authenticate its human, and negotiate permissions, but other systems execute and audit.

This also explains the Throne's preference for proxies. He cannot directly satisfy every authorization condition, so he shapes a person whose own identity and beliefs make the desired request appear independent.

## 4. The system should preserve both safety and liveness

Distributed-systems research distinguishes **safety**—preventing conflicting or invalid outcomes—from **liveness**—eventually allowing valid action to complete. Strong protections can preserve safety by freezing action, but a civilization that never resolves conflict becomes unusable.

The endgame can exploit this tension:

- Sylvan can prevent the Throne from obtaining a clean unilateral outcome.
- The Throne can prevent Sylvan from securing immediate full disclosure.
- George's contested status blocks automatic intervention.
- Different authorities possess valid but incompatible claims.
- The system escalates toward a public process because private resolution has deadlocked.

The public phase change is then a governance fallback, not a magical reward. When concealed negotiation cannot produce a legitimate result, the system widens the authorized audience and makes claims auditable.

## 5. Influence should be multidimensional

Instead of “Sylvan has 70 influence and the Throne has 65,” use several forms:

- **personal standing:** accumulated reliability under known conditions;
- **domain authority:** competence or office within a particular system;
- **relational trust:** delegated power from other people or agents;
- **plan support:** backing attached to a specific proposal;
- **institutional mandate:** authority held through a role;
- **protective priority:** system weight assigned to vulnerable people or irreversible harm;
- **adversarial discount:** reduced weight because of containment status or detected manipulation;
- **public legitimacy:** recognized support that becomes harder to falsify after phase change.

These can conflict. The Throne may have enormous relational and institutional leverage inside his faction while carrying severe hidden containment restrictions. Sylvan may have less direct authority but stronger evidentiary standing and wider cross-faction trust.

## 6. Strategy emerges from the rules

Mechanism design studies how rules shape behavior when participants have different preferences and may act strategically. No aggregation system is neutral or immune to gaming. A mature civilization therefore expects strategic conduct and structures incentives around it.

The Throne's methods can be understood as governance exploits:

- gather delegated authority through certification;
- redefine private interests as institutional requests;
- use genealogy to create dependent claimants;
- split harmful plans into individually permissible steps;
- manufacture apparent consent;
- route action through George's credentials;
- exploit lag between hidden evidence and recognized status.

Sylvan counters by changing the inputs the system recognizes:

- authenticate evidence;
- revoke compromised delegation;
- create common knowledge;
- assemble independent approvals;
- prove conflicts of interest;
- force high-consequence actions into auditable public procedure.

## 7. Why neither side can simply kill the other

A coherent protection stack might include:

- participants have a baseline protection status;
- direct lethal action requires an immediate-threat finding;
- a personal agent cannot be the sole verifier of its human's threat claim;
- evidence from compromised sources receives reduced weight;
- high-level participants possess reciprocal vetoes over irreversible intervention;
- unexplained disappearance triggers archival and public-phase safeguards;
- indirect manipulation accumulates anomaly flags but is hard to prove event by event;
- local violence remains possible through ordinary actors and existing machinery.

This preserves danger. The system blocks effortless elimination but cannot prevent every cascading, deniable, or emergent harm.

## 8. George as an authorization exploit

George is useful because he possesses attributes the father lacks:

- apparent independent threat perception;
- legitimate public roles;
- a long record of service;
- a Daemon able to submit authenticated requests;
- personal history with Sylvan;
- standing not visibly marked as the father's direct command.

The father's plan is to make George's attack pass as a genuine participant conflict. Evidence of lifelong manipulation changes the system's interpretation from “independent action” to “proxy action through a compromised agent.”

George's awakening can therefore affect permissions in real time. Once he contests his own authorization or identifies his Daemon as compromised, the father loses the channel he spent decades constructing.

## 9. Scene-level applications

- The Throne has physical access to a system but receives a denial because the affected person's agent objects.
- Sylvan's request is valid yet stalled because one required role is held by an implicated institution.
- George learns that revoking his own credentials will prevent the attack but permanently end his public authority.
- Two agents expose contradictory threat claims, forcing escalation to a wider tribunal.
- The father fragments one forbidden plan into many ordinary administrative decisions.
- Public phase changes which evidence may be discussed and who can authorize review.
- A synthetic institution follows George's orders until his provenance chain reveals paternal control.

## 10. Guardrails

- Rules should create dilemmas, not solve scenes automatically.
- Distinguish hard constraints from weighted preferences.
- Every safeguard should have a cost, latency, or possible exploit.
- Avoid presenting social choice as mathematically neutral.
- Do not let “the system” make moral judgments the characters must make.
- Preserve ordinary physical and political action beneath advanced governance.

## Creative decisions this research can unlock

1. What baseline protection prevents direct elimination?
2. Which actions require multiple independent approvals?
3. Which attributes make George's attack appear legitimate?
4. What event triggers public-phase escalation?
5. Which authority remains under the Throne's control after exposure?
6. What does George permanently lose by revoking his own standing?

## Bottom line for *Seeds of the Throne*

The system should not ask who is strongest. It should ask which action is authorized under contested identity, evidence, jurisdiction, consent, and risk. That turns the climax into a struggle over legitimate coordination while leaving human choices morally decisive.

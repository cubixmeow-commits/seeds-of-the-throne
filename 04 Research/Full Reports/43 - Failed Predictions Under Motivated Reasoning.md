# 43 — Failed Predictions Under Motivated Reasoning

**Story focus:** P-02 The Failure Ledger; P-07 Konrad's First Unscripted Verification

## Research question

Which forms of prediction logging, precommitment, and repeated comparison make discrepancies difficult to rationalize away for a committed believer?

## Bottom line

A bare list of failures is weak. A credible failure ledger needs **predictions recorded before outcomes**, operational success criteria, time windows, preserved revisions, comparable observations, and a scoring or adjudication rule that the believer cannot rewrite after the fact.

Research does not show that such a ledger irresistibly changes a motivated person's mind. Mixed or ambiguous evidence can be assimilated selectively, although clearer evidence can produce genuine updating. The dramatic value is therefore not a magical conversion. It is that each new explanation must visibly contradict an earlier commitment, raising the personal and social cost of continued denial.

## Supported findings

### 1. Strong prior beliefs can distort evaluation of mixed evidence

**[SUPPORTED]** Classic biased-assimilation work found that people with opposing commitments evaluated the same mixed evidence differently, accepting supportive material more readily and criticizing contrary material more aggressively. Later research cautions that belief perseverance is not absolute: clear, coherent counter-evidence can shift beliefs.

**Story value:** the ledger should reduce ambiguity rather than merely increase the volume of evidence.

### 2. Tracking and scoring improve forecast accountability

**[SUPPORTED]** Good Judgment Project research found that probability training, collaboration, and performance tracking improved calibration and resolution. Forecasting tournaments make claims comparable by defining questions, deadlines, outcomes, and scoring rules in advance.

**Story value:** Samuel cannot safely replace “the attack will decisively stop Sylvan” with “the attack taught us something useful” after the outcome unless the change is recorded as a retreat.

### 3. A forecast should remain updateable without erasing its history

**[SUPPORTED / PRACTICE INFERENCE]** Skilled forecasting permits revisions as new information arrives, but quality control depends on preserving when a forecast was made, what information was available, and how accuracy is scored. An append-only history distinguishes legitimate updating from retrospective rewriting.

**Story value:** Konrad can watch Samuel move probabilities, definitions, and causal stories over time. The incriminating pattern is not that Samuel ever updates; it is that every update protects the same preferred conclusion after the stated test fails.

### 4. Incentives for accuracy can reduce motivated belief

**[SUPPORTED]** Experimental work on motivated beliefs finds that accuracy incentives can mitigate motivated reasoning. This does not erase identity-protective reasoning, but it suggests that consequences tied to prior predictions can change how evidence is processed.

**Story value:** a later attack decision can require Konrad to put authority, reputation, or a protected claim behind the prediction instead of treating each attempt as costless.

## Minimum credible ledger fields

1. claimant and decision authority;
2. timestamp and evidence available at the time;
3. predicted observable outcome;
4. probability or confidence band;
5. deadline or observation window;
6. success, failure, and ambiguous-result criteria;
7. actual observation and its provenance;
8. independent verification or known blind spots;
9. forecast revisions preserved rather than overwritten;
10. explanation offered after the result, linked to what it contradicts.

## Research-to-story translation

`pre-outcome claim + fixed observation window + append-only revisions + independently visible result -> failed attacks cannot be narratively reset -> Konrad must authorize another test while knowing that every revision strengthens Sylvan's model`

### Strong upgrade: the prediction lock

Before a major attack, Samuel must state what observable change will count as success. Konrad privately preserves the statement through a channel Samuel believes he controls. Afterward, Sylvan and Konrad independently compare the promised effect with the same bounded observations. The scene does not prove every hidden cause; it proves that Samuel's explanation is becoming less predictive.

## Constraints and failure modes

- If Sylvan defines all criteria, the ledger can look adversarially rigged.
- If outcomes are inherently ambiguous, the ledger may intensify polarization.
- A single dramatic failure is easier to excuse than a repeated comparable series.
- Scoring must not imply perfect observability.
- Legitimate model updates must remain possible; otherwise the system punishes learning.
- Konrad should not become reasonable merely because a chart exists. The choice remains identity-threatening.

## What this adds to the creative packet

- P-02 should use precommitted success thresholds and an append-only explanation history.
- P-07 can be the act that creates an independently preserved forecast rather than merely changing an attack parameter.
- The public reveal can show **definition drift**—how the promised outcome repeatedly changed after failure—rather than a generic montage of unsuccessful attacks.

# When Accommodation Produces Escalation

## Purpose

This report examines when inclusion, negotiation, concessions, recognition, prosperity, or limited authority can produce moderation—and when they instead increase a destructive movement's confidence that its larger goal is achievable. The report avoids assuming that all radical, criminal, or authoritarian groups behave alike.

## Executive finding

Accommodation is most likely to support peace when parties can make credible commitments, internal constituencies reward compliance, gains do not automatically expand coercive capacity, and violations produce reliable consequences.

It becomes dangerous when concessions change an actor's estimate of eventual victory without changing the actor's objective. The key diagnostic is therefore not whether an offer is generous. It is whether the offer changes incentives, capabilities, internal legitimacy, and beliefs about what future pressure can obtain.

## 1. Negotiation can work

Research on bargaining with armed and extremist organizations does not support a universal rule that negotiation always fails. Agreements become more plausible when monitoring and enforcement make commitments credible, when intermediaries can impose costs, and when parties can control their own factions.

For the colonization process, genuine cooperation requires more than a leader's promise. It requires:

- observable behavior;
- internal ability to enforce the agreement;
- benefits that depend on continued compliance;
- independent verification;
- consequences neither side can selectively suspend;
- a path for followers who prefer peace over the leader's maximal goal.

## 2. Movements contain factions

Concessions can strengthen moderates, but they can also provoke extremist outbidding or allow leaders to radicalize followers who threaten their position. A contained faction should not be treated as one mind.

Relevant internal groups may include:

- true believers who value the final goal above present welfare;
- opportunists who benefit from movement institutions;
- followers seeking safety or belonging;
- moderates willing to accept durable limits;
- leaders whose personal authority depends on permanent conflict;
- defectors who need protection before speaking honestly.

An offer aimed at “the group” can have opposite effects on each constituency.

## 3. Concessions communicate information

Every accommodation changes beliefs. A concession may be interpreted as:

- respect that makes peaceful participation legitimate;
- evidence that negotiation works;
- proof that coercion produces rewards;
- confirmation that the opponent lacks resolve;
- an opportunity to acquire resources for the next phase;
- a face-saving exit from a failing strategy.

The meaning depends on timing, public explanation, enforcement, and the movement's internal narrative. Identical material terms can moderate one actor and embolden another.

## 4. Credible limits change the calculation

Boundaries are effective when they are known, behaviorally defined, consistently enforced, and paired with a real cooperative path. Vague threats and discretionary punishment invite testing.

A strong arrangement distinguishes:

- beliefs, traditions, and internal identity, which remain protected;
- external conduct, which is observable and bounded;
- benefits earned through sustained cooperation;
- automatic consequences for renewed predation;
- review procedures for disputed facts.

This supports Sylvan's emerging principle: he need not redesign the faction's identity. He needs to stop destructive conduct from producing additional leverage.

## 5. Prosperity alone does not settle political objectives

Material improvement can reduce grievances, but leaders motivated by status, domination, sacred duty, revenge, or historical destiny may not substitute prosperity for political victory. Resources can increase capability even while improving daily life.

The process should therefore test whether benefits produce:

- reduced recruitment into coercive structures;
- willingness to accept permanent limits;
- truthful reporting of violations;
- protection of dissenters;
- constructive institution-building;
- decreased dependence on enemy narratives.

Comfort without behavioral change is not evidence of moderation.

## 6. Century-scale repetition can reveal strategy

One failed agreement proves little. Over many environments, the system can observe whether concessions repeatedly follow the same pattern:

**recognition -> access -> institutional foothold -> capture -> coercion -> external attack -> demand for greater recognition**

The important evidence is not merely recurrence. It is adaptive behavior showing that leaders learned how to use prior accommodations more effectively while preserving the same objective.

The record must also test alternative explanations. Poorly designed agreements, humiliating implementation, unreliable enforcement, or genuine betrayal by authorities can cause failure without proving that accommodation itself is futile.

## 7. What would count as genuine change

Indicators of moderation should be costly and behavioral:

- accepting a limit when violating it would be advantageous;
- disciplining powerful insiders for breaches;
- permitting followers to leave;
- protecting critics and inconvenient evidence;
- relinquishing captured authority when terms require it;
- cooperating when no immediate reward is available;
- revising public narratives that make future aggression inevitable.

Private statements of peaceful intention are weaker than institutions that remain peaceful when leadership changes.

## Failure modes

- Authorities mistake silence for moderation.
- Concessions reward the faction most capable of disruption.
- Hard boundaries become collective punishment.
- Moderates are exposed without protection.
- The system interprets every violation as proof that change is impossible.
- Leaders intentionally prolong conflict because their internal legitimacy depends on it.
- Observers evaluate intentions while ignoring increased capacity.

## Creative questions

1. Which accommodation most clearly increases the Throne's estimate that victory is possible?
2. Which follower constituency genuinely wants a durable settlement?
3. What costly action would prove the faction has changed?
4. Which boundary remains credible even when enforcing it is politically inconvenient?
5. How does the Reconciliation School distinguish a failed offer from an actor exploiting the offer?

## Bottom line for *Seeds of the Throne*

The century-long process should not prove that mercy is foolish. It should reveal the conditions under which mercy becomes peace and the conditions under which it becomes evidence, resources, or confidence for continued expansion. The danger begins when destructive behavior repeatedly changes the terms in its favor.

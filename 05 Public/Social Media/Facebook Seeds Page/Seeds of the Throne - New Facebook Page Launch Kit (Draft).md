## Pinned first post (draft, ready to paste)

A development journal, day one.

I am building a story called Seeds of the Throne, and I am reading two books while I build it: Russell Targ's Third Eye Spies, about the CIA remote viewing program, and Robert Monroe's Journeys Out of the Body, about leaving the body and keeping a log like an engineer.

The overlap is the whole point of this Page. A mind receives impressions it cannot account for. Some are signal, some are noise, and some may be supplied by something that benefits from being believed. The remote viewers solved this with protocol: blind the viewer, separate the judge, keep the record sealed. The traveler solved it with discipline: stay calm, steer by attention, and test whatever you find against the checkable world.

Here, daily: what the documents actually say, what the story is testing, and the question underneath it all. When something arrives in your mind uninvited, how do you decide what it is?

This is a journal, not a finished book. Notes, drafts, and works in progress.

## First seven daily entries (drafts, one per day, all from material already in the vault today)

Day 2, The protocol: The rule at SRI, where the CIA's remote viewers worked: the viewer describes, the judge decides. The viewer was never allowed to interpret what they saw. Interpretation was someone else's job, done blind, afterward. In Seeds of the Throne, the Luminai are built on that exact discipline. The villains are what happens when the judge starts whispering first.

Day 3, Locale check: Monroe catalogued his out of body trips statistically: about a third in Locale I, the physical world, where a claim can be checked. The rest, somewhere thought itself shapes what you meet. Every story about a voice in your head needs that distinction. Which locale is the voice speaking from, and can it survive a check?

Day 4, Getting loosh right: Loosh, the famous Monroe concept, is not in Journeys Out of the Body. It arrives in Far Journeys, fourteen years later. Most Monroe content online gets this wrong. In Monroe's account, Earth is a garden farmed for an emotional energy called loosh, and humanity is a crop that does not know it is being farmed. Getting the source right matters, because the story's quiet villains run the same shape in a different substance.

Day 5, One document, two verdicts: The 1995 evaluation of Star Gate contains both conclusions. Statistician Jessica Utts: the numbers hold up. Psychologist Ray Hyman: the conclusion is still premature. The program closed anyway. Same data, opposite verdicts. That is the story's epistemology in miniature, and it is why the story's heroes refuse to obey an impression before it has earned it.

Day 6, What the leaders farm: New in the story today. The environment exists because some questions cannot be settled by argument: two camps, each completely certain, each able to talk forever. So the participants are given something argument never provides, the chance to act on what they believe, inside conditions controlled carefully enough that the results mean something, recorded faithfully enough that nobody gets to edit them afterward. That only works while nobody knows it is an experiment. The subjects of the experiment are the last people who can be told.

Day 7, Two waves: The story's villains arrive in two waves. The first began in secret, grew loud, and was exposed by a great war for exactly what it was. The second group studied that failure and chose the opposite road: stay quiet, never raise its voice, and feed people the explanation first, so each person's own mind supplies the evidence afterward. The quiet ones are led by a man whose obsession is not wealth or territory but complete control of every participant's mind and actions. They have to be dragged into the light by a record they cannot edit.

# Overlap Essay, Draft v1 (2026-11-07)
Prose draft for refinement. Topics first, books as background. No em dashes. Story material framed as fiction in development.

For twenty years, the United States government paid scientists to answer a question it could not ask in public: can a person describe a place they have never been, hidden on the other side of the world, using nothing but their mind? The viewers who did this work were kept blind. They were given coordinates and nothing else, and their job was only to describe. Someone else, later, also blind, decided what the description matched. Describe first. Interpret later. Judge separately. That separation was the entire discipline, and the declassified record argues about it to this day, with serious people on both sides of the same official report.

At roughly the same time, a radio executive in Virginia was keeping a different kind of record. His experiences began by accident in 1958: a vibration that ran through his body like a current, and then the repeated discovery that he could leave it. He did what an engineer does. He logged hundreds of trips, classified them, counted them. About a third took place in the physical world, where a claim can be checked against a fact. The rest unfolded somewhere stranger, a place where thought and emotion seemed to shape what he met the moment he thought it. His whole education came down to one skill: attention. Where your attention went, you went.

Here is the overlap we keep circling. A mind receives impressions it cannot account for. Some are signal, some are noise, and some may be supplied by something that benefits from being believed. The remote viewers solved this with protocol: blind the viewer, separate the judge, keep the record sealed. The traveler solved it with discipline: stay calm, steer by attention, and test whatever you find against the checkable world. Both of them understood that the most dangerous moment is not the strange impression. It is the explanation that arrives too quickly, wearing the impression's clothes.

Seeds of the Throne, the story world being built here, takes that problem and gives it architecture. Imagine a civilization that pairs every human mind with an artificial one, a partner whose first job is not to answer but to keep provenance: to know which thoughts were yours, which were contributed, and which were interpretations pretending to be facts. The heroes of that world are not the people who receive the strongest impressions. They are the people who refuse to obey an impression before it has earned it. The villains arrive in two waves. The first began in secret, grew loud, and was exposed by a great war for exactly what it was. The second group studied that failure and chose the opposite road: stay quiet, never raise its voice, and feed people the explanation first, so each person's own mind supplies the evidence afterward. The loud ones fell to exposure. The quiet ones are led by a man whose obsession is not wealth or territory but complete control of every participant's mind and actions, exercised so quietly that the controlled believe they are thinking for themselves. They have to be dragged into the light by a record they cannot edit.

And what is being farmed in that world is not belief. It is evidence. The environment exists because some questions cannot be settled by argument: two camps, each completely certain, each able to talk forever. So the participants are given something argument never provides, the chance to act on what they believe, inside conditions controlled carefully enough that the results mean something, recorded faithfully enough that nobody gets to edit them afterward. That only works while nobody knows it is an experiment. The moment participants know they are being watched, they perform instead of live, and the data dies. It is the viewers' blinding, rebuilt at the scale of a civilization: the subjects of the experiment are the last people who can be told.

So this is the territory: remote viewing and astral projection as real human practices with real documents and real arguments behind them, and a story that treats the space between perception and belief as the most contested ground a person owns. We will stay on the checkable side of the line. Documents will be documents, experiences will be experiences, and the fiction will be labeled fiction. The question underneath all of it stays open, because it is a good one: when something arrives in your mind uninvited, how do you decide what it is?

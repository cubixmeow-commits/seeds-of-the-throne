# Development Prototype Style

## Purpose

This is the default **development-story rendering mode** for Seeds of the Throne.

It exists because the project currently needs readable story simulations more often than finished-novel prose.

Use it to turn outlines, events, mechanisms, characters, and alternate possibilities into one or two pages that are easy to read and easy to judge.

The prototype should feel like a real story, but it is disposable development material.

## Primary goal

**Make the idea easy to experience.**

The author should be able to read the prototype quickly and answer:

- Is this situation interesting?
- Do these characters work together?
- Is the conflict understandable?
- Does the mechanism make sense in motion?
- Does the reveal feel earned?
- Would I want to continue this version?

## Target qualities

Heavily favor:

- fast readability;
- clear immediate objectives;
- conversational rhythm;
- visible problem-solving;
- simple scene geometry;
- distinct character attitudes;
- light orientation reminders;
- technical explanation through dialogue/action;
- humor or personality when natural;
- short paragraphs;
- clean causal turns;
- concrete actions;
- a changed situation by the end.

Do not optimize for literary density.

Do not make every paragraph carry thematic symbolism.

Do not hide basic scene information merely to create mystery.

## Typical length

Default:

**500–1,500 words**

Short tests may be 250–500 words.

A compressed chapter prototype may extend to roughly 2,000 words when necessary.

Prefer several cheap prototypes over one prematurely polished 5,000-word draft.

## Scene engine

A useful default structure is:

`orientation -> immediate problem -> character reaction -> proposed solution -> disagreement/test -> result -> complication -> changed state`

Not every prototype needs every beat.

The structure exists to keep development material active.

## Paragraph style

Prefer:

- mostly short to medium paragraphs;
- dialogue separated clearly;
- one major action or idea per paragraph when possible;
- occasional longer paragraph only when the listener/reader has a stable anchor;
- periodic concrete nouns after abstract discussion;
- explicit speaker/context restoration if dialogue becomes complex.

## Dialogue

Dialogue is a major development tool because it exposes whether characters actually have different minds.

Give each speaker:

- a different immediate goal;
- a different interpretation of events;
- a different tolerance for risk;
- a different level/type of knowledge;
- at least one small behavioral or verbal tendency.

Do not create gimmick voices or repeated catchphrases merely to differentiate speakers.

## Exposition

Use exposition only when someone needs it.

Preferred:

`question -> explanation -> objection -> demonstration -> consequence`

or:

`unexpected event -> argument over cause -> test -> partial answer`

Avoid encyclopedia paragraphs unless the prototype specifically exists to test an exposition passage.

## Problem-solving tone

Characters should be allowed to think in front of the reader.

Useful actions:

- compare possibilities;
- notice a constraint;
- reject an obvious solution;
- propose a workaround;
- test a prediction;
- recognize an error;
- exploit a small advantage;
- realize that success created a new dependency.

The audience should be able to participate mentally without needing specialist knowledge.

## Humor and levity

Development prototypes may use more overt humor and conversational looseness than final Seeds prose if that helps reveal character chemistry.

Humor should come from:

- incompatible personalities;
- absurd circumstances;
- professional frustration;
- misplaced confidence;
- practical responses to enormous events;
- status conflict;
- a character noticing what everyone else is pretending not to notice.

Do not copy recurring jokes, comedic personas, or distinctive banter from reference works.

## Supporting characters in prototypes

A temporary character does not need a full biography before appearing.

They need enough specificity to become memorable immediately:

- role;
- immediate desire;
- competence or useful knowledge;
- one contradiction;
- one behavioral detail;
- relationship to the viewpoint character;
- reason they matter in this scene.

See [[08 Story Loop/CHARACTER-FACTORY]].

## Audio-first readability

Even in text development, assume the prototype may eventually be heard.

Check:

- Can a listener identify who is speaking?
- Are long noun stacks avoided?
- Are names introduced at a manageable rate?
- Does the scene remind the listener what matters after a digression?
- Are causal transitions explicit enough to hear once?
- Can the current objective be summarized in one sentence?

## What this style is NOT

It is not:

- final manuscript voice;
- a replacement for Seeds prose rules;
- permission to make the story shallow;
- a requirement that every scene be comedic;
- an imitation of Craig Alanson or any other living author;
- a reason to preserve prototype prose after the structural idea has served its purpose.

## Development freedom

Because these are prototypes, repetition across separate tests is acceptable.

It is fine to reuse useful structures such as:

`problem -> workaround -> consequence`

across many development samples.

The concern about pattern fatigue belongs primarily to later manuscript assembly.

During development, clarity and comparability are more valuable.

## Default prototype prompt logic

When asked to render a development idea as story:

1. retrieve only the relevant canon and constraints;
2. identify viewpoint;
3. state the immediate scene objective internally;
4. choose 2–4 active characters maximum unless crowd dynamics are being tested;
5. open close to the problem;
6. expose worldbuilding through use;
7. create at least one meaningful decision or test;
8. end after a state change;
9. label all newly invented details as PROPOSED in accompanying notes;
10. do not canonize the prose itself.

## Output package

A useful development output may include:

### Prototype
The readable scene/chapter sample.

### What this version tested
2–5 bullets.

### New assumptions introduced
Any details invented to make the scene function.

### What worked
Short structural observations.

### What failed / needs author decision
Short structural observations.

This separates story judgment from prose attachment.

## X adaptation

The same readability principles can support X development posts:

- clear setup;
- one striking problem/reversal;
- conversational explanation;
- minimal prerequisite knowledge;
- strong final turn.

X output should be compressed separately rather than copied directly from a prototype.

## Success condition

A successful development prototype is not beautiful enough to publish.

It is **clear enough, alive enough, and story-like enough to reveal whether the underlying idea deserves further development.**

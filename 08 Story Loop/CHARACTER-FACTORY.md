# Character Factory

## Purpose

Create characters at the level of detail the story currently needs, especially the many supporting and temporary characters required across centuries of colonization, the Great War, containment, advanced-environment development, and later arcs.

The system should avoid two opposite failures:

- cardboard function-characters;
- wasting development effort on full biographies for people who appear briefly.

## Character tiers

### Tier A — Anchor characters

Examples include Sylvan, Samuel, George, Konrad, Orzai, and other major long-range figures.

Develop deeply:

- formative history;
- contradictions;
- relationships;
- worldview;
- recurring needs and obsessions;
- developmental arc;
- long-range causal role;
- speech/behavior under different pressures;
- how they change across eras.

### Tier B — Arc characters

Important for one substantial era, institutional arc, war campaign, containment phase, or multi-chapter sequence.

Need:

- clear backstory relevant to present conflict;
- relationship web;
- personal stakes;
- competence;
- contradiction;
- specific pressure point;
- arc beginning/change/end;
- memorable behavioral texture.

### Tier C — Recurring supporting characters

Appear intermittently and can become audience favorites without carrying the entire story.

Need:

- occupation/function;
- immediate worldview;
- relation to anchor character;
- one strength;
- one vulnerability or contradiction;
- one recognizable habit or preference;
- one unresolved personal concern;
- reason they plausibly reappear.

### Tier D — Scene characters

Appear for one or a few scenes.

Need only enough detail to feel like a person rather than a delivery device.

Minimum six-point packet:

1. **role** — why are they in this environment?
2. **want** — what do they want right now?
3. **competence** — what are they good at or unusually aware of?
4. **contradiction** — one thing that prevents stereotype flattening;
5. **behavioral detail** — one small concrete trait;
6. **story function** — why does this scene need them?

Example form:

`rail coordinator / wants the evacuation slot protected / excellent at capacity planning / ideologically loyal but privately hates ceremonial interference / counts available cars under her breath / forces Konrad's officers to choose between symbolism and logistics`

That is often enough to produce readable dialogue.

## The temporary-character formula

For fast generation use:

`social role + immediate want + competence + contradiction + pressure + small human detail`

Then add:

`relationship to viewpoint + what changes because of them`

The character should not be defined by appearance alone.

## Memorable without gimmicks

Temporary characters become enjoyable when the reader can infer that they have a life outside the scene.

Useful signals:

- they are already busy with something when the protagonist arrives;
- they have an opinion unrelated to the protagonist;
- they care about a small practical consequence;
- they have a professional standard;
- they disagree with someone on their own side;
- they possess incomplete information and act rationally from it;
- they remember a prior inconvenience;
- they want to leave, eat, finish work, protect someone, avoid embarrassment, keep a promise, or solve a mundane problem.

## Fast backstory generation

Backstory should explain present behavior, not exist for completeness.

For Tier C/D characters, generate only:

- one relevant past event;
- one current obligation;
- one relationship outside the immediate scene;
- one belief formed from experience.

Do not automatically generate childhood, parents, schooling, romantic history, or complete chronology unless required.

## Voice differentiation

Differentiate through **thinking and priorities** before dialect or verbal gimmicks.

Possible dimensions:

- terse vs explanatory;
- cautious vs improvisational;
- literal vs political;
- technically precise vs socially perceptive;
- deferential vs status-insensitive;
- impatient vs procedural;
- optimistic vs experienced pessimism;
- notices people vs notices systems.

Use recurring phrasing sparingly.

## Character-generation loop

When a story structure needs characters:

`story function -> role candidates -> select plausible social position -> generate six-point packets -> test interaction -> critic pass -> deepen only survivors`

Generate several options when the role materially changes the scene.

## Supporting-character critic

Ask:

- Could this person be replaced by a generic official without changing anything?
- Do they want something besides delivering exposition?
- Are they competent enough to deserve their position?
- Does their behavior follow from what they know?
- Do they create pressure or choice?
- Is the contradiction subtle enough to feel human rather than quirky by formula?
- Would one detail make them recognizable when they return twenty chapters later?
- Do they accidentally duplicate another character's function?

## Character promotion

A Tier D character can become Tier C or B if story generation discovers unusual value.

Promotion signs:

- strong chemistry with an anchor character;
- unique access to a recurring system;
- unexpected thematic contrast;
- naturally recurring institutional role;
- audience-facing emotional value;
- a choice with major downstream consequences.

Do not pre-plan every promotion.

## Character retirement

Not every likable character needs a larger arc.

Some should:

- complete their function;
- transfer away;
- survive and simply stop being relevant;
- die when causally justified;
- reappear much later;
- remain an unresolved historical trace.

Long stories feel larger when not everyone stays attached to the protagonist forever.

## Colonization-era application

The long colonization process can support dozens of vivid temporary and recurring people across:

- terraforming;
- synthetic deployment;
- governance;
- logistics;
- research;
- military operations;
- early settlements;
- command infrastructure;
- witness/archive roles;
- containment administration;
- developmental-participant support;
- Great War institutions;
- postwar processing;
- modern advanced-environment jobs.

This allows Seeds to feel populated without requiring dozens of equally deep protagonists.

## Success condition

The Character Factory succeeds when the development system can create a new supporting cast for a chapter or era quickly, make them enjoyable enough to test in story form, and deepen only the characters who prove they deserve more narrative space.

# Research and Questioning

## Purpose

Use questions and research to remove uncertainty that would materially improve a scene. Do not turn preparation into a substitute for writing.

## Classify the unknown

Before asking or researching, classify it:

### A. Blocking story decision
Examples: who betrays whom, whether George knows a hidden fact, what an unresolved technology is canonically capable of.

Do not invent it. Route to `develop-story-session` or surface the question outside the prose.

### B. Researchable real-world detail
Examples: how a hearing proceeds, how an archive is cataloged, what a physical environment feels like, how a specific technology or institution plausibly behaves.

Route substantial external research to `research-story-material`. Frame the research around scene consequences rather than general background.

### C. Low-risk connective detail
Examples: a chair position, a cup on a table, a hallway turn, a clerk's minor action, weather that does not alter canon.

In an exploratory draft, invent cautiously when needed for scene coherence and mark material invented details as proposed afterward.

### D. Craft uncertainty
Examples: where the scene should begin, whether dialogue or narration should carry a fact, how close the POV should be.

Use the prose modules or `coach-seeds-writing`. Do not ask the author to solve a craft problem the skill can reasonably test itself.

## Question discipline

Ask before drafting only when an unresolved choice would substantially change the scene and no safe exploratory version can be produced. Prefer one high-leverage question over a questionnaire.

When a best-effort draft is possible, draft first and surface up to three consequential open questions afterward.

A good question exposes a choice with dramatic consequences. A weak question asks the author to specify incidental details the writer could safely handle.

## Research packet

When research is needed, request only what can enter the scene. Define:

1. the scene situation;
2. the factual uncertainty;
3. the character action or consequence it affects;
4. the level of realism required;
5. what would falsify the preferred assumption;
6. the maximum useful detail.

Example framing: "What visible steps, delays, records, and opportunities for human discretion would appear during this kind of institutional audit?" is better than "Research audits."

## Translation from research to prose

Research is advisory. Convert it into:

- constraints;
- sensory/procedural details;
- credible failure modes;
- choices available to characters;
- costs and delays;
- vocabulary a character would plausibly use.

Do not paste research explanation into dialogue or narration merely because it is accurate.

## Stop rule

Research has done enough when the writer can make the current scene physically and causally credible. Additional interesting facts belong in notes, not automatically in the scene.

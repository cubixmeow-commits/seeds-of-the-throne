# Suspense and Revelation

## Information map

For important scenes track four columns mentally:

- what the reader knows;
- what the POV knows;
- what another character knows;
- what each party falsely believes.

Suspense often comes from the gaps, not from withholding everything.

## Suspense modes

- **Threat suspense**: reader anticipates a danger the character may or may not see.
- **Mystery suspense**: character and reader seek an explanation.
- **Dramatic irony**: reader understands a meaning the character misses.
- **Interpretive suspense**: facts are visible but their meaning remains disputed.
- **Procedural suspense**: a process, audit, vote, test, arrest, disclosure, or institutional mechanism moves toward an outcome.
- **Relational suspense**: a character must decide whom to trust, betray, protect, or expose.

## Reveal ladder

Good reveals often progress through:

1. anomaly;
2. plausible local explanation;
3. second fact that weakens it;
4. pattern;
5. consequence;
6. reinterpretation.

Do not rush from anomaly to total explanation.

## Foreshadowing

Plant details that are independently natural in the scene. A clue should preferably serve a present function before it serves a future reveal.

Avoid conspicuously ominous objects or lines that exist only to announce importance.

## The turn versus the reveal

A **reveal** adds or confirms information. A **turn** changes the meaning of information already present. They can coincide but should not be treated as synonyms.

## Restraint

Do not end every section with a twist. Suspense also survives through unresolved cost, difficult choices, partial evidence, and consequences whose meaning is still developing.

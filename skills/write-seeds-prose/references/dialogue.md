# Dialogue

## Dialogue is behavior

Every important line should do at least one job for the speaker: obtain, avoid, test, dominate, reassure, recruit, threaten, bargain, conceal, provoke, misdirect, confess, stall, or refuse.

## Before writing an exchange

Know:

- what each speaker wants from the other;
- what each refuses to say directly;
- what each believes the other knows;
- where their status differs;
- what would make one of them change tactics.

## Preferred behavior

- Let characters answer the pressure, not the exposition prompt.
- Allow partial answers, interruptions, deflections, wrong assumptions, and strategic silence.
- Use action beats when they alter interpretation or spatial logic, not as decorative fidgeting.
- Distinguish voices through priorities, syntax, tolerance for ambiguity, status behavior, and habitual tactics rather than catchphrases.
- Let dangerous people sound ordinary when ordinary language better reveals their worldview.

## Exposition in dialogue

A character may explain when they have a reason to explain: instruction, persuasion, interrogation, justification, deception, briefing, correction, testimony, negotiation, or argument.

Do not let characters tell each other mutually known facts solely for the reader.

## Political grotesque

For senior contained leadership, favor euphemism, procedure, petty grievance, hierarchy, inheritance, administrative language, and personal vanity over operatic evil. The moral horror should often be clearer to the reader than to the speaker.

## Dialogue revision test

For each major line ask:

1. Why is this speaker saying this now?
2. What response are they trying to produce?
3. Could the same information be revealed by conflict, omission, or consequence instead?
4. Would this person actually phrase it this way?
5. Does the line change the exchange?

If a line exists only because the reader needs lore, redesign the beat.

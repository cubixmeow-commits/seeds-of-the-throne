# Character Voice

## Principle

Character voice is not a bag of catchphrases. It is the repeated shape of attention, motive, status behavior, assumptions, vocabulary, and tactics under pressure.

## Build a voice card

For each important POV or speaker, derive from current character notes and approved prose:

- **Attention bias:** what this person notices first.
- **Default interpretation:** what they assume events mean before evidence forces change.
- **Status behavior:** how they speak upward, downward, and laterally.
- **Conflict tactic:** confront, charm, evade, intellectualize, joke, threaten, bargain, recruit, withdraw, proceduralize, etc.
- **Emotional concealment:** what they refuse to display and what leaks anyway.
- **Vocabulary domain:** institutions, family, systems, evidence, physical craft, ideology, commerce, military language, etc.
- **Sentence tendency:** clipped, cumulative, qualified, declarative, digressive, interrogative, restrained.
- **Tolerance for uncertainty:** whether they need closure, enjoy ambiguity, or weaponize it.
- **Self-story:** the explanation they prefer for their own behavior.
- **Blind spot:** what their voice systematically fails to name or see.

Do not invent a permanent trait because one scene needs it. Mark inferred voice traits as working until supported.

## POV voice

Narration in close POV should inherit the character's attention and assumptions without becoming literal internal monologue every sentence. The world can be described objectively enough to remain readable while selection and interpretation remain character-shaped.

## Dialogue differentiation

Different characters should not become distinct merely by slang or verbal tics. Test by removing dialogue tags: differences should emerge from what each person wants, what they consider relevant, how directly they answer, and what social risks they notice.

## Voice under pressure

A strong voice changes without disappearing. Under stress:

- a controlled person may become shorter, not suddenly poetic;
- a vain person may become more procedural when exposed;
- a frightened person may focus obsessively on a practical detail;
- a manipulator may become unusually direct when their usual tactics stop working.

Use pressure to reveal the limits of the normal voice.

## Cross-register rule

Archive reconstruction may describe a character differently from that character's close POV. Do not confuse historian interpretation with the character's self-understanding.

## Voice drift check

After drafting ask:

1. Could another major character say these lines unchanged?
2. Does the POV notice what this person would notice?
3. Did the prose give the character knowledge or moral clarity they do not possess?
4. Did a project-wide style habit overwrite individual voice?
5. Is any repeated verbal tic becoming a caricature?
